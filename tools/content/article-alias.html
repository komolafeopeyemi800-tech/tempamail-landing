If you want to stop giving your real email address to every website, you have more options than "make a throwaway Gmail account". Temporary inboxes, forwarding aliases, plus-addressing and custom-domain catch-alls all solve the same problem in different ways, and each fits a different situation. Choosing the wrong one is how people end up locked out of accounts or still drowning in spam. This guide compares them honestly, including where our own service is the wrong answer.

Key takeaways

  • Temporary inbox: best for one-off sign-ups you will never need again. Nothing reaches your real inbox and everything is deleted.
  • Forwarding alias: best for accounts you want to keep, because mail is forwarded to your real inbox and you can still reset passwords.
  • Plus-addressing: free and built into some providers, but it only labels your real address and does not hide it.
  • Use your real address only for banks, government, work and people you know.

The four approaches

1. Temporary (disposable) inbox

You get a random address at a domain run by a service, with an inbox you can read in a web page. There is no account, and the inbox and its messages are deleted automatically (after 24 hours here). Nothing is forwarded to you; you read mail on the service's page. This is what Tempamail.org provides, and it is described in our introduction to temporary email.

Strengths: instant, no sign-up, nothing to clean up, no link to your identity. Weaknesses: it is temporary, so you cannot reset a password later; the inbox is readable by anyone who knows the address; some sites block disposable domains.

2. Forwarding alias (email masking)

An alias service gives you a unique address for each website. Mail sent to the alias is forwarded to your real inbox, and the website never sees the real address. If an alias starts attracting spam, you switch it off and the junk stops. Well-known options include Firefox Relay, Apple's Hide My Email (part of the paid iCloud+ subscription), DuckDuckGo Email Protection and SimpleLogin. Plans, prices and free limits change, so check each provider's current page before you choose; for example, Mozilla's help page lists a cap on how many masks the free plan allows.

Strengths: works for long-term accounts, because you still receive password-reset emails; easy to see which site leaked or sold your address; can be disabled at any time. Weaknesses: needs an account with a provider, which then knows your real address; free plans have limits; some sites also block known alias domains; if the provider shuts down, aliases stop working.

3. Plus-addressing and dot variations

Some providers, including Gmail, deliver mail sent to yourname+shopping@gmail.com to yourname@gmail.com. You can put any label after the plus sign and filter on it. It is free and requires no extra service.

Strengths: zero setup; handy for filtering and for tracing who sold your address. Weaknesses: it does not hide anything. Anyone can remove the "+label" part and get your real address, and spammers' tools do exactly that. Some websites reject addresses containing a plus sign.

4. Your own domain with a catch-all

If you own a domain, you can accept mail for any name at it and create a new address, such as amazon@yourdomain.com, for every service. This is how Tempamail.org itself is built. It is flexible and under your control, but it costs a domain, takes technical setup, and mixes everything into one place unless you separate it.

Which one should you use? Five common situations

You want a free PDF or a discount code

Use a temporary inbox. You need one message, and you will never log in again.

You are creating a social media or shopping account you will keep

Use a forwarding alias (or your real address if you trust the service). You will need password resets and order emails for years, so the address must keep working.

You want to know who sold your address

Use a different alias or plus-label for each company. When spam arrives at yourname+newsletter@... you know where it came from.

You are building or testing software

Use temporary inboxes for manual testing of sign-up and confirmation emails, or a catch-all on a domain you control for automated tests.

It is a bank, tax office, employer or healthcare provider

Use your real, secured address with two-factor authentication. Never use a temporary address here; it exposes you to account lockout and, because anyone with the address can read it, to privacy and security risks. See when to use a disposable email and when not to.

Things that trip people up

  • Forgetting where you signed up. With aliases and temporary inboxes, keep a note (a password manager entry works) of which address you used for which site.
  • Mixing purposes. If you use an alias for something important and then lose access to the alias service, you can lose the account too. Keep important accounts on addresses you can always reach.
  • Assuming an alias makes you anonymous. It hides your email address, but the site still receives your IP address, device information and anything you enter. Also, aliases do not stop phishing; read our guide to spotting phishing emails.
  • Trying to defeat site rules. Using any of these tools to claim a limited offer repeatedly or evade a ban may violate that site's terms. We do not encourage it.

A simple system that works

  1. Tier 1, real address: banking, government, work, family. Never share it elsewhere.
  2. Tier 2, alias or secondary address: shopping, social media, newsletters you actually want.
  3. Tier 3, temporary inbox: everything you only need once.

This three-tier setup takes ten minutes, costs nothing and means the address most worth protecting stays off most mailing lists. If an address does leak, you can find out using the steps in how to check whether your email was in a data breach.
