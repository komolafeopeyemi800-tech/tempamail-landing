9 Practical Ways to Cut Spam and Protect Your Real Inbox

Simple habits that reduce spam and phishing: separate addresses, safer sign-ups, breach checks, and when a disposable email is the right tool.

Last updated November 8, 2026

Most spam does not arrive by accident. Your address ends up on a list because it was typed into a sign-up form, published on a web page, or leaked in a data breach. Once it is on a list it is copied and resold, and removing it is nearly impossible. The practical approach is to control where your real address goes in the first place. These nine habits are simple and do not need any paid tools.

1. Use a different address for different purposes

Keep one address for people you know, a second for shopping and accounts, and a third for everything else. If the shopping address starts receiving junk, you have lost one address, not your whole digital identity. Most email providers let you create extra addresses or aliases for free.

2. Do not publish your address in plain text

Automated programs scan websites, forums and social profiles for anything that looks like an email address. If you need to show one publicly, use a contact form, an image, or a dedicated public address that you expect to be noisy.

3. Read the tick boxes before you sign up

Many forms include a pre-ticked box that agrees to marketing email or to "share with partners". Untick them. It takes two seconds and prevents a surprising amount of unwanted mail.

4. Use a disposable address for one-off sign-ups

For a free download, a one-time coupon or a site you will never visit again, there is no reason to give out your real address. A temporary email address gets you the confirmation code while keeping your real inbox off the list. Just remember the limits: do not use one for anything you may need to recover later. 5. Be careful with "unsubscribe" links

For newsletters from companies you recognise, the unsubscribe link is safe and usually required by law to work. For unsolicited mail from unknown senders, do not click it. Replying or unsubscribing can confirm that your address is real and active, which makes it more valuable to spammers. Mark such messages as spam instead.

6. Check whether your address has appeared in a breach

The free service Have I Been Pwned lets you check whether an address appears in known data breaches. If it does, change the password for that service, and for any other site where you reused it. Treat unexpected mail that mentions that service with extra suspicion for a while.

7. Learn the signs of phishing

  • An urgent deadline ("act within 24 hours or your account will be closed")
  • A sender address that looks almost right but is not, such as a swapped letter in the domain
  • A link whose real destination, shown when you hover over it, does not match the company it claims to be from
  • A request for a password, payment details or a one-time code

When in doubt, do not use the link in the email. Open the company's website yourself or use its official app.

8. Turn on two-factor authentication for important accounts

Spam is annoying, but a stolen email account is serious, because it can be used to reset your other passwords. Use a unique, long password for your main address and turn on two-factor authentication, ideally with an authenticator app or a security key rather than text messages.

9. Train your spam filter and report what slips through

Use the "report spam" or "report phishing" button rather than just deleting. It teaches your provider's filter and helps protect other people. Avoid opening attachments or images from senders you do not know.

What these habits will not do

None of this will remove you from lists you are already on, and no single trick stops spam completely. The aim is to reduce the amount that reaches you and to make sure that if one address is compromised, the damage is small. Start with habits 1, 3 and 4: separate addresses, careful tick boxes and a throwaway address for one-off sign-ups prevent most of the problem at the source.
