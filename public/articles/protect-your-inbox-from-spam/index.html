9 Practical Ways to Cut Spam and Protect Your Real Inbox

Nine habits that reduce spam and phishing: separate addresses, safer sign-ups, when unsubscribing is safe, breach checks and filters.

By Tempamail.org Editorial TeamUpdated November 8, 20268 min read

Most spam does not arrive by accident. Your address reaches spammers because it was typed into a sign-up form, published on a web page, shared by a company you trusted, or leaked in a data breach. Once an address is on a list it is copied and resold, and getting it off every list is practically impossible. The sensible strategy is therefore to control where your real address goes and to make the mail that does arrive easier to deal with. These nine habits are free, take little time, and work whichever email provider you use.

Key takeaways

  • Prevention beats cleanup: stop giving your main address to sites that do not need it.
  • Use the unsubscribe link only on mail from companies you recognise. For anything else, mark it as spam.
  • Legitimate bulk senders are legally required to honour opt-outs, but spammers ignore the law, so do not click their links.
  • Keep one carefully protected address for banking and important accounts.

1. Use different addresses for different purposes

Keep one address for people you know and for banks and government services, a second for shopping and accounts, and a third for everything else. If the shopping address starts receiving junk, you have lost one address rather than your entire online identity. Most providers let you create extra addresses or aliases for free; our comparison of temp mail and aliases shows the options.

2. Do not publish your address in plain text

Automated programs scan web pages, forums, code repositories and social profiles for anything that looks like an email address. If you must show one publicly, use a contact form, an image, or a dedicated public address that you expect to be noisy. Never put your primary address in a public profile.

3. Read the tick boxes

Many forms include a pre-ticked box that agrees to marketing email or sharing details with "partners". Untick it. Two seconds of care here prevents a surprising amount of unwanted mail later.

4. Use a disposable address for one-off sign-ups

For a free download, a one-time coupon or a website you will never visit again, there is no reason to give out your real address. A temporary email address gets you the confirmation code or link and then expires, so the website never receives anything it can market to. Remember the limits: never use one for accounts you may need to recover, a point we explain in when to use a disposable email and when not to.

5. Know when unsubscribing is safe

For newsletters and promotions from companies you recognise, the unsubscribe link is the right tool. In the United States, the CAN-SPAM Act requires commercial email to include a clear way to opt out and requires the sender to honour the request within ten business days; the statute (15 U.S.C. § 7704) sets out the rules. Many other regions, including the EU under GDPR and the ePrivacy rules, have similar or stricter requirements. Large mailbox providers have also tightened their rules for bulk senders in recent years, which has made one-click unsubscribe more common.

For unsolicited mail from senders you do not know, do not click unsubscribe and do not reply. Genuine companies obey the law, but spammers do not, and a response only confirms that your address is active and read. Mark the message as spam instead.

6. Check whether your address has appeared in a breach

If your address is in a leaked database, spam and phishing aimed at it are likely to increase. The free service Have I Been Pwned lets you check any address and sign up for alerts. If it appears, change the password on the affected service and anywhere you reused it. Our full walkthrough is in how to check whether your email was in a data breach.

7. Learn to recognise phishing

A lot of spam is really phishing: messages that pretend to be a bank, courier or employer to steal your login. Warning signs include urgent deadlines, a sender address that does not match the company, generic greetings, and links whose real destination differs from the button text. If in doubt, do not use the link; open the website yourself. We show an annotated example in how to spot a phishing email.

8. Protect the account itself

Spam is annoying, but a stolen email account is serious because password-reset links for your other accounts go there. Give your main address a long, unique password and turn on two-factor authentication, ideally with an authenticator app, passkey or security key rather than text messages.

9. Train your spam filter

Use the "report spam" or "report phishing" button rather than simply deleting a message. It improves your provider's filter for you and for other users. Create filters for the addresses or plus-labels you use for sign-ups so that wanted mail lands where you expect it. Avoid opening attachments or loading images from senders you do not know; remote images can confirm to a sender that your address is live.

If your inbox is already flooded

If spam is already a daily problem, work through it in order rather than deleting messages one by one:

  1. Find the source. If you use plus-labels, aliases or separate addresses, look at which one the junk is addressed to. That tells you which company leaked or sold the address, and you can close that route.
  2. Block at the address, not the sender. Spammers rotate sender addresses constantly, so blocking each one is endless. If an alias or secondary address is the target, disable or delete it.
  3. Check for forwarding rules. A hijacked account sometimes has a hidden rule forwarding your mail elsewhere. Look in your mail settings for filters or forwarding addresses you did not create.
  4. Move important contacts first. If an address is beyond saving, create a new one, update your bank, work and family, and let the old one run down.

Most people never need to abandon an address entirely, but knowing it is an option removes the pressure to tolerate a hopeless inbox.

A realistic expectation

None of this will remove you from lists you are already on, and no single trick stops spam completely. The aim is to reduce the amount that reaches you and to make sure that if one address is compromised, the damage is limited. If you do only three things, make them these: separate addresses, careful tick boxes, and a throwaway address for one-off sign-ups. They prevent most of the problem at the source.
