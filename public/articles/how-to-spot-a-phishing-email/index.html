How to Spot a Phishing Email: Red Flags and What to Do

An annotated example of a phishing email, the warning signs to look for, what to do if you already clicked, and habits that make you harder to trick.

By Tempamail.org Editorial TeamUpdated November 8, 20268 min read

Phishing is the oldest trick in online crime and it is still one of the most effective, because it does not attack software. It attacks attention. A message arrives that looks like it came from your bank, your delivery company or your employer, creates a feeling of urgency, and asks you to click or reply before you think. According to the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, scammers use email or text messages to trick people into giving up personal information such as passwords, account numbers or Social Security numbers. The good news is that most phishing messages share a handful of tell-tale signs, and learning them takes about ten minutes.

Key takeaways

  • Phishing messages rely on urgency, fear or a too-good-to-be-true offer. Slow down whenever a message pushes you to act fast.
  • Check the real sender address and the real link destination, not the display name or button text.
  • Never use a link in an unexpected message to log in. Open the official website or app yourself.
  • If you already clicked or typed a password, change it immediately, turn on two-factor authentication and report the message.

Why phishing works

Phishing succeeds for human reasons, not technical ones. We read dozens of messages a day on small screens, often while doing something else. Attackers copy the logo, layout and tone of a trusted company and add a reason to act now: a locked account, an unpaid invoice, a parcel that cannot be delivered, a prize. Under pressure the careful part of the brain switches off. Every habit in this guide exists to put a small pause back in.

The anatomy of a phishing email

The example below is fictional, but it contains the elements you will meet again and again. Each numbered marker is explained underneath.

  1. The sender's address does not match the company. The display name says "Northbank Security", but the actual address ends in northbank-secure-login.example, a domain the attacker registered. Always look at the part after the @, not the friendly name.
  2. The subject line creates urgency. Deadlines of hours rather than weeks, words such as "urgent", "final notice" and "action required" are designed to stop you from checking.
  3. The greeting is generic. Real banks usually use your name or a partial account detail. "Dear customer" suggests a bulk message.
  4. The link goes somewhere else. Hover over a button (on a phone, press and hold) to see the real address. Here northbank.com appears at the start to fool you, but the real site is whatever comes right before the first single slash: verify-account-help.example.
  5. The message threatens a loss. Fear of losing money or access is the engine of most phishing.

Other signs worth knowing

  • Unexpected attachments, especially invoices, scanned documents and "voicemails" that you did not ask for.
  • Requests for secrets. Legitimate companies do not ask for your password, full card number or one-time code by email, text or phone.
  • Mismatched context. A parcel notice when you ordered nothing, a bank you do not use, a boss asking for gift cards.
  • Poor formatting or odd wording. This used to be the main giveaway; writing tools have made it less reliable, so do not treat clean grammar as proof of safety.
  • Pressure to keep it secret or to bypass normal process ("do not call the office, just pay").

What to do when a message looks suspicious

  1. Stop. Do not click, reply, scan a QR code or open an attachment.
  2. Check independently. Open the company's official website by typing the address yourself, or use its official app, and look for the same notice there. Or call the number printed on your card or bill.
  3. Report it. In the U.S. you can report phishing to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Most email apps also have a "report phishing" option that trains the filter and protects other people.
  4. Delete it. Once reported, remove the message so you are not tempted later.

What if you already clicked?

It happens to careful people. The FTC advises that if you think you clicked a link or opened an attachment that could have installed harmful software, you should update your computer's security software and run a scan. In addition:

  • If you typed a password, change it at once on the real site, and everywhere else you used it. Then turn on two-factor authentication.
  • If you shared card or bank details, call your bank or card issuer using the number on the card and ask them to block and replace it.
  • If you shared a Social Security or national ID number, visit IdentityTheft.gov (U.S.) for a recovery plan, and consider a credit freeze.
  • Watch for follow-ups. Someone who has your details may try again with a more convincing message.

Habits that make you harder to phish

  • Use a password manager. It fills passwords only on the correct website, so a fake login page gets nothing. That alone defeats many attacks.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication, preferably with a passkey or security key, which are designed to resist phishing, rather than text-message codes.
  • Use separate addresses. A dedicated address for shopping and a temporary address for one-off sign-ups means a phishing email sent to the wrong address is instantly suspicious, and it keeps your main inbox out of marketing lists that scammers buy.
  • Keep software updated and use your email provider's built-in spam and phishing protection.

Does temporary email protect against phishing?

Partly. A throwaway address reduces how many lists your real address lands on, which reduces how much phishing reaches you. It does not make you immune: a phishing page can still steal whatever you type into it, and a message sent to a temporary inbox can contain a malicious link just like any other. Treat links in temporary inboxes with the same suspicion as anywhere else. For a broader plan, read 9 practical ways to cut spam and how to check whether your email was in a data breach.
